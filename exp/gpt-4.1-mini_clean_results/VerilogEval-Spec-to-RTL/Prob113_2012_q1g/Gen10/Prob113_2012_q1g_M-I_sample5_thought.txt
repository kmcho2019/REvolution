The previous solution is already minimal in terms of sum-of-products logic derived from the K-map. However, it has some redundancy because terms 2 and 3 both include x[2], and term 3 is a subset of term 2 in some cases. We can factor common signals to reduce the number of AND gates and potentially reduce power and area by reducing total gate count.

Specifically, note:

- term2 = x[2] & ~x[0]

- term3 = x[2] & ~x[3] & ~x[1]

So both have x[2] ANDed with other terms. We can factor x[2]:

f = (~x2 & ~x3 & ~x1) | x2 & ( ~x0 | (~x3 & ~x1) ) | (x2 & x3 & x0 & x1)

Further, the last term (x2 & x3 & x0 & x1) is actually a subset of x2 & x3 & x0 & x1, which may or may not be covered by the factored expression.

Check if the last term is covered by x2 & ( ~x0 | (~x3 & ~x1) ):

At last term x2=1,x3=1,x0=1,x1=1:

- ~x0 = 0

- ~x3 = 0

- ~x1 = 0

So (~x0 | (~x3 & ~x1)) = 0

Not covered, so keep last term.

Rewrite f as:

f = (~x2 & ~x3 & ~x1) | x2 & ( ~x0 | (~x3 & ~x1) ) | (x2 & x3 & x0 & x1)

This saves one AND gate from term3 by factoring x2 outside.

In implementation, compute:

- nx0 = ~x[0]

- nx1 = ~x[1]

- nx3 = ~x[3]

- nx2 = ~x[2]

- term1 = nx2 & nx3 & nx1

- term2_3 = x[2] & ( nx0 | (nx3 & nx1) )

- term4 = x[2] & x[3] & x[0] & x[1]

Then f = term1 | term2_3 | term4

This reduces gate count and levels slightly, potentially improving power and area without impacting performance.

Additionally, re-use inverted signals to avoid duplication.

This factoring maintains correctness and logic minimality.
