Instead of directly instantiating two A and two B modules independently and combining their outputs with gates, this new design leverages shared intermediate computations and a structural reuse approach.

We know:
- Module A implements z = (x ^ y) & x.
- Module B's output pattern suggests it's related to ~((x^y) & x) or a function close to B's observed waveform.

In this new approach:

1. Instantiate two A modules with inputs (x,y), obtain A1_out and A2_out.
2. Instantiate two B modules, but instead of directly connecting them to inputs (x,y), cascade them such that:
   - The first B receives inputs (x,y) directly.
   - The second B receives as inputs a logic combination of (x,y) influenced by A1_out to model different behavior, e.g., B2's inputs are modified by A1_out.
   
3. Then combine outputs:
   - OR gate combines A1_out and B1_out.
   - AND gate combines A2_out and B2_out.
   - XOR the OR and AND outputs for final z.

This architecture increases functional diversity of the two B submodules by feeding B2 different inputs influenced by A1_out. This allows the top-level output z to produce richer patterns potentially matching the specified waveform while still respecting instantiation count constraints.

By having B2's inputs dependent on A1_out and original inputs, the design introduces controlled combinational diversity, potentially simplifying logic if B2 can be optimized to only handle certain input patterns.

This structure:
- Maintains two A and two B submodule instantiations.
- Uses input sharing and inter-submodule dependencies to reduce redundancies.
- Exploits logic diversity by varying B inputs to allow simplified logic inside B.
- Keeps the top-level combinational nature for zero clock period and minimal delay.

Overall, this new architectural approach uses input modification for B2 to increase combinational differentiation between the two B modules, which can help reduce redundant gate duplication and possibly achieve better power and area by optimizing B modules individually.
