The original solution correctly implements the required logic but has redundancy by instantiating duplicate A and B modules with identical inputs, resulting in duplicated computations and increased area and power.

Since all four submodules share the same inputs (x, y), and modules A and B implement pure combinational logic that depends only on these inputs, their outputs will be identical in both instances. Hence, we can instantiate each module only once and reuse their outputs.

This reduces the number of submodules from 4 to 2 (one A and one B) and simplifies the top-level logic accordingly: The first OR gate combines the single outputs from A and B, the AND gate also combines the same outputs (since originally it connected second A and B outputs, identical in this case), then the XOR of these OR and AND outputs produces z.

This approach reduces area and power by eliminating duplication without changing the logical behavior. It also reduces logic gate count and interconnect, potentially improving timing.

If the problem explicitly requires two instances each of A and B, but inputs are identical, we can tie their outputs together and avoid separate computations.

Hence, optimization focuses on logic sharing and duplication removal.
