This time, instead of decoding states explicitly, let's use a truth table approach focusing solely on the bits of y and w to derive next y[1] directly via a simple combinational logic expression without named intermediate states.

We know:

- Input y[2:0] and w determine the next state.
- We only need to find the next value of y[1].

From the FSM transitions, we derive the next y1 for each state and input:

State | y2 y1 y0 | w=0 | w=1 | next y1 (w=0) | next y1 (w=1)
A     | 0  0  0 | B(001) | A(000) | 0 | 0
B     | 0  0  1 | C(010) | D(011) | 1 | 1
C     | 0  1  0 | E(100) | D(011) | 0 | 1
D     | 0  1  1 | F(101) | A(000) | 0 | 0
E     | 1  0  0 | E(100) | D(011) | 0 | 1
F     | 1  0  1 | C(010) | D(011) | 1 | 1

Let's note the binary inputs: y2,y1,y0,w and the next y1:

We want to express next y1 as a function of y2, y1, y0, and w.

We see next y1 = 1 when:
- B (0 0 1), any w
- C (0 1 0), w=1
- E (1 0 0), w=1
- F (1 0 1), any w

Expressing these conditions logically:

- B = ~y2 & ~y1 & y0
- C = ~y2 & y1 & ~y0
- E = y2 & ~y1 & ~y0
- F = y2 & ~y1 & y0

Next y1 = B + (C & w) + (E & w) + F

To avoid decoding states explicitly, notice B and F both are (~y1 & y0) but differ on y2, so we can group B and F:

B + F = (~y1 & y0 & (~y2 | y2)) = ~y1 & y0

So next y1 simplifies to:

next_y1 = (~y1 & y0) | (w & ((~y2 & y1 & ~y0) | (y2 & ~y1 & ~y0)))

We'll implement this expression directly, minimizing gate count and avoiding explicit state names.

This approach uses only boolean expressions derived directly from the bits, optimizing for compactness and clarity, without intermediate signals.

Finally, output Y1 = next_y1.
