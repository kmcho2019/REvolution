Both examples correctly interpret the Karnaugh map and leverage the don't-care conditions to simplify the logic.

Example 1 directly grouped terms, yielding: out = a | (c & ~a & ~b) | (~c & ~d & ~a & b), explicitly using d in one product term.

Example 2 ignored d mostly (since d is don't-care), resulting in: out = (a & ~c) | (c & (~b | a)), which is a nicely factored expression without explicit dependence on d, leading to minimal area and power.

To fuse the best of both:

- Use Example 2's factoring, which is clean and efficient.

- Include d explicitly only if it offers a clear simplification, else rely on the fact that d is don't-care and can be ignored to simplify implementation.

- Factor the expression further to share terms and minimize gate levels for better timing.

Notably, Example 2's expression:

    out = (a & ~c) | (c & (~b | a))

can be factored as:

    out = a | (c & ~b)

because:

    (a & ~c) | (c & a) = a & (~c | c) = a

So,

    out = a | (c & ~b)

This is a simpler and more minimal expression with fewer literals and no explicit d.

This expression:

- outputs 1 if a=1 regardless of c,b,d

- outputs 1 if c=1 and b=0 (regardless of a,d)

This aligns well with the K-map after assigning don't-care values to maximize grouping.

This simplification uses only a, b, c and does not depend on d, which matches the requirement that d is don't-care and thus can be ignored.

This expression is minimal and can be implemented with a single OR of a and (c & ~b), which is optimal for area, power, and performance.

Therefore, the superior solution is:

    out = a | (c & ~b)

This simple and elegant expression benefits from:

- Minimum gate count and fan-in

- No dependency on d input, simplifying routing and logic

- Clear interpretation from K-map simplification
