To fuse the best ideas from both examples, the strategy is as follows:

- From Example 1, using arithmetic addition leverages synthesis tools' ability to optimize adders efficiently, possibly producing very optimized balanced logic, with minimal delay and area.
- From Example 2, explicit parity and majority logic offers minimal, clear logic gates, likely minimal area and power for such a small input width.

To get a superior solution, we combine the clarity and minimalism of Example 2's explicit parity and majority logic with the clarity and synthesis friendliness of Example 1's addition approach.

Specifically, we implement addition of the bits explicitly but extract the bits of the sum manually to reveal the parity and majority signals directly, confirming the output bits match:

- out[0] (LSB) = parity = sum[0] (LSB of sum)
- out[1] (MSB) = majority = sum[1] (MSB of sum)

Since addition of three 1-bit inputs produces a 2-bit sum representing population count (0 to 3), this matches perfectly.

Writing this in a way that synthesis tools can still optimize efficiently is best done by direct addition as in Example 1, but annotated with comments showing the equivalence to parity/majority logic. This approach is very concise, synthesis-friendly, and minimal in logic depth.

Hence, the fused solution is:

- Use out = in[0] + in[1] + in[2]; as a single arithmetic operation.
- Add comments explaining how this corresponds to parity and majority.
- This preserves correctness, minimal delay, minimal power and area, and clarity.

This fusion captures the strength of both approaches: the minimal logic of explicit parity/majority and the synthesis-friendly efficient addition.

Therefore, the final design is to implement the population count with direct addition, relying on synthesis optimization, and documenting the parity and majority equivalence in comments.
