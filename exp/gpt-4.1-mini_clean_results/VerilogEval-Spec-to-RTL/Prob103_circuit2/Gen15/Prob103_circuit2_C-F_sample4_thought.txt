Both examples identify that the output q represents the even parity of inputs a, b, c, and d, implemented as the negation of the XOR of all inputs. Example 1 uses `assign q = ~(a ^ b ^ c ^ d);` while Example 2 uses `assign q = ~^(a ^ b ^ c ^ d);`. Both are functionally equivalent.

To fuse their strengths:
- Use the reduction XOR operator `^` directly on the vector of inputs to make the code more scalable and readable.
- Apply the negation (`~`) outside the reduction XOR for clarity.
- Group inputs as a vector `{a,b,c,d}` for compactness and ease of extension.
- Keep the implementation purely combinational with a continuous assign for minimal timing and area.

This approach combines the concise, scalable syntax from Example 2 with the explicit clarity and standard form in Example 1, leading to a clean, efficient, and maintainable RTL code.

Hence, the fused implementation is:
`assign q = ~(^ {a, b, c, d});`

This clearly shows q is the even parity bit of the inputs.
