To implement a novel fixed-point subtractor, I propose an architecture that leverages a combination of shift operations and adder/subtractor logic to handle the subtraction of two fixed-point numbers while maintaining precision defined by the parameters Q (fractional bits) and N (total bits). The approach involves separating the integer and fractional parts of the inputs, performing subtraction on these parts separately, and then recombining the results while handling sign and precision considerations.

The key idea is to use two separate adder/subtractor units: one for the integer part and one for the fractional part. The input numbers are first separated into their integer and fractional components based on the value of Q. The integer parts are subtracted using one adder/subtractor, and the fractional parts are subtracted using another. The results from these operations are then combined, taking care to handle any potential carry from the fractional part subtraction into the integer part.

To handle different sign subtractions and maintain precision, additional logic is included to detect the signs of the inputs and adjust the operation accordingly. If the signs are different, the absolute values are subtracted, and the sign of the result is determined based on which input is larger. For same-sign subtractions, the signs are the same as the inputs. The result is then constructed by concatenating the integer and fractional parts, ensuring that the total number of bits N is maintained.

The use of separate adder/subtractor units for integer and fractional parts, along with explicit handling of signs and precision, allows for a modular and scalable design that can be easily parameterized for different values of Q and N. This approach also enables easier verification and testing of the module, as the separate handling of integer and fractional parts can be independently validated.

In terms of synthesizability, this design should be efficient since it primarily involves basic arithmetic and logical operations that are well-supported by most digital design tools. The separation of logic into integer and fractional parts also allows for potential pipelining or parallelization in more complex designs, which can improve performance in certain applications.
